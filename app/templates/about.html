Privacy & limits

Privacy, science and limits

What this prototype is, what it is not, and how it handles data.

01What it does

It detects changes in task interaction timing compared with each person's own usual pattern, and flags changes that may merit a human check-in before a critical operation.

02What it does not do
  • It does not detect or diagnose fatigue, illness, or cognitive impairment.
  • It does not clear anyone for duty or control any mission decision.
  • Its thresholds are illustrative prototype choices, not NASA-approved or clinically validated limits.
03Data handling
  • Only aggregate timing numbers are stored (see the preview shown before every submission).
  • Typed text, key identities and raw keystroke timelines never leave the browser. The server rejects them.
  • Monitoring runs only inside the task, with a visible indicator.
  • Demo crew members are fictional; their data is synthetic and labelled as such.
04Retention and access

This is a proposal, not something the prototype does. The prototype has no accounts: anyone who can open it can see every profile, and data is kept until the demo database is reset.

  • The crew member sees their own numbers first, and every flag about them.
  • Only the reviewer named for the operation sees a crew member's flags and evidence. Nobody else, by default.
  • Keep only what the baseline needs. It already uses at most the 20 most recent comparable sessions, so older sessions add nothing and could be deleted.
  • A crew member can ask for their data to be deleted, and it is.
  • Record who accepted a new normal, and when. Today only the time is stored, because there are no accounts.
  • The retention period and the reviewer role are for the crew and the mission's operations team to decide, not for us.
05Other reasons timing changes

Timing can change for many ordinary reasons. The prototype cannot tell them apart and does not try; that is why a flag leads to a conversation, and a person decides.

  • Keyboard. A new or different keyboard changes typing rhythm. If the change is lasting, a reviewer can accept it as the person's new normal.
  • Language. The checklist phrases are in English. Typing in a second language, or switching keyboard layouts, changes timing.
  • Time of day. A session at an unusual hour can differ from a baseline recorded at another time.
  • Sleep. A short or disrupted night can change timing. The prototype measures no sleep data and says nothing about how someone slept.
  • Practice. People get faster as a task becomes familiar. Being faster than usual is never flagged, but early sessions make a baseline that later sessions may drift away from.
06What we validated

On simulated data only: a benchmark of simulated crews with injected changes whose timing and size we know, two scoring methods measured on the same people (300 simulated people per row, 40 sessions each).

  • Fewer false alarms. At the alert level the product uses, false alerts on normal sessions fall from about 13.0 % to about 4.7 %.
  • A trade-off. Detection also falls at that level: for a 10 % slowdown, 0.42 to 0.35 of affected sessions flagged.
  • Better at matched false-alert rates. With false alerts held at 5 %, for a 10 % slowdown: 0.24 to 0.36 when pauses are Poisson-like, 0.22 to 0.29 when they are very bursty.
  • A flaw found and fixed. In a sustained 20 % slowdown, the old method learned the change as normal and detection fell from 0.88 to 0.07 by the fifteenth affected session. Keeping flagged sessions out of the baseline held it at 0.40.

Details and the commands to reproduce them are on the Evidence page.

07What we did not validate
  • No real people. We have no data from astronauts, crews or volunteers. The demo crew is fictional and its data synthetic.
  • No licensed public dataset. We found none with enough repeated sessions per person and a licence we could cite, so we did not use one.
  • Our own simulator. We wrote the simulator and the model, so the benchmark is partly circular. Testing several pause patterns and a wrong assumption reduces that but cannot remove it.
  • Modest gains. The improvement is real and reproducible, not a breakthrough, and part of it is a trade of sensitivity for fewer false alerts.
  • Total pause time is no longer scored, so one very long pause can go unflagged. It is shown as context.
  • Accepting a new normal depends on a reviewer's judgement and is not benchmarked.
  • No clinical or operational claim. Thresholds are illustrative. Nothing here detects fatigue or illness, clears anyone for duty, or is approved or endorsed by NASA.
